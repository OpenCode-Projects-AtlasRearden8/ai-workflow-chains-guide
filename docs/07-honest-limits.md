# Honest Limits

*What chaining can't do — said plainly, because trust needs this chapter.*

## The big one: a chain is not a fact-checker

Chains multiply the *organization* of your work. They do not multiply its
**truth**. If step 1 confidently states something wrong, steps 2 and 3 will
build on it with equal confidence — a chain doesn't know it's wrong either.
A polished five-step chain weaving one mistaken premise produces a
professional-looking mistake.

What actually guards truth in a chain:

- **The CHECK lines.** Read every upstream answer like a skeptic before
  pasting it forward. That interrupting human glance is the only lie detector
  in the loop.
- **Fresh facts come from sources, not the model.** Prices, dates, policies,
  medical stuff, legal stuff — a text model's recall may be out of date,
  fuzzy, or confidently invented. When a step needs a fact, get it from the
  real source and *paste it in as material*. Models chain nicely around the
  facts you supply; they shouldn't be the supplier.
- **Cheap friction helps.** Ask the chain to "list which claims in the notes
  above you cannot verify" and then verify those few things yourself. You
  don't need to doubt everything — just the named suspects.

## Accuracy decays with depth

Each handoff costs a little fidelity — summaries drop nuance, weak models
misread dense pastes, small wording shifts change downstream tone. A chain
gets less reliable **with each link**, not more. Practical ceilings from
experience:

- **3–4 steps**: comfortably reliable for everyday jobs (this manual's range).
- **5–7 steps**: fine with real CHECK discipline and a condensing step or two.
- **10+ steps**: you're building a process now, not using one — reconsider
  whether some steps belong *outside* the AI entirely.

## Where chains are simply the wrong tool

- **Anything where a single number decides it.** If the job is arithmetic —
  a budget with real sums, a mortgage comparison — use a calculator or
  spreadsheet for the math and use the chain around the *decisions*.
  Ask politely for "just the arithmetic, double-checked" and still spot-check
  the total yourself.
- **Personal facts the model can't know.** Your energy levels, your kid's
  schedule, what your boss meant. The AI can structure those; it cannot know
  them. If the chain output feels hollow, it's usually missing *your* facts —
  paste more of them in.
- **Certainty-hungry decisions.** Big-money, health, and legal choices can
  use a chain for *preparation* (gathering questions, organizing the options)
  but never for the verdict. A chain is a brilliant tax on your own thinking
  and a terrible authority. Professionals exist; use one.
- **New things that are truly emergent.** A one-of-a-kind creative leap
  doesn't chain well; the steps resist being predefined. Chains are for
  *repeatable shapes* of jobs. That's also why they're wonderful for the
  everyday ones.

## The privacy ceiling — one more time

Chapter 1's postcard rule bears repeating where it bites: small steps plus
stronger contexts can tempt pasting more, not less. Resist. Sensitive
material — identity documents, credentials, health details, other people's
personal info — does not go into cloud prompts, chained or not. If a step
needs that material, consider a local model for that step (chapter 4), or
restructure the chain so the sensitive part happens in your head. Chains
don't get a privacy exemption for being helpful.

## What chaining is actually good at

Worth stating plainly, since the limits page shouldn't only be doom:

- Making **scattered jobs** feel like three small ones.
- Separating gathering from judging, so both are done on purpose.
- Creating checkable seams — places where a non-expert can catch drift.
- Being saved, improved, shared, and re-run — every run pays a little rent
  back into the tool.

Chains won't think for you. They make it easy to *notice your own thinking*
— and being able to see your steps is most of the benefit anyway.
